Where the stacking-order disorder actually comes from

Reading time 6 min · saved to a local library

The devices were exfoliated the usual way, and the trouble showed up only after the second anneal: a disorder in the layer sequence that no one had introduced on purpose. Raman maps put it right under the edge of the flake, which is exactly where the polymer residue collects.

What matters for a transport measurement is that the disorder is not uniform. It sits in the first two layers, leaves the bulk of the channel alone, and therefore biases any average you take over the whole device. The authors report the shift in the charge-neutrality point, but not its spread, and the spread is the part that decides whether a hysteresis window is a property of the material or of your lithography.

We kept the same flakes for a week. Nothing degraded, nothing cleaned up either.

The practical reading is unglamorous. Disorder is not a number you report in the caption of a figure; it is a constraint on what the device can tell you. Once you accept that, the experiment gets shorter, because half of the questions you were going to ask are already answered by the metrology you skipped.

We now image the edge before we bond the gate, which costs an afternoon and saves a month. The reviewer who asked whether the effect survives a second device is, as usual, the one who was right.
