Good morning. In 2004, a team in Manchester asked a simple question: can we build a transistor out of a metal? Not a semiconductor, a metal. I want to show you why that question mattered, and how they answered it.

The obstacle is easy to state. An electric field dies out within about a nanometre inside a metal, so a metal film has to be atomically thin before a gate can reach its carriers. But films that thin had always been unstable: they break up into islands. As the authors put it, no metal or semimetal had shown a field effect larger than one percent.

Their answer was [[few-layer graphene]]. They took [[highly oriented pyrolytic graphite]] and peeled it with adhesive tape, again and again. That is [[mechanical exfoliation]]. The flakes landed on a silicon wafer covered with 300 nanometres of [[silicon dioxide]], which served as the gate dielectric. Then they patterned [[Hall bars]] into the flakes and measured transport.

So what did they see? The [[sheet resistance]] rises to a peak of a few kilohms near zero gate voltage, and falls to about a hundred ohms at high gate voltage. The conductivity is linear in gate voltage on both sides of that peak, and the [[Hall coefficient]] changes sign at exactly the same voltage. That is the [[ambipolar field effect]]: the same film conducts with electrons on one side, with holes on the other, and with both near the peak. This is not a semiconductor with a gap. It is a two-dimensional [[semimetal]] with a tiny band overlap of a few millielectronvolts.

The numbers are what made people pay attention. Gate voltages of about a hundred volts induced carrier densities up to ten to the thirteenth per square centimetre, with room-temperature [[mobility]] of order ten thousand square centimetres per volt-second. The [[mean free path]] was about 0.4 micrometres, so transport was [[ballistic]] on submicrometre scales.

Film quality also showed up in [[Shubnikov-de Haas]] oscillations. Their frequency was linear in gate voltage, the signature of a two-dimensional [[density of states]], not the three-dimensional behaviour of graphite.

Two honest caveats. The on-off ratio stayed below thirty at room temperature, because there is no gap to switch off. And the as-made devices were hole-doped by adsorbed water; annealing in vacuum moved the peak back to zero volts. So the intrinsic state is a mixed-carrier semimetal, not a clean zero-gap crystal.

The takeaway is this. A single atomic layer can be a working electronic material, and a gate can move its Fermi level across both carrier types. Everything that followed, from [[quantum Hall]] effects to [[encapsulation]] and [[twistronics]], starts from this measurement.

Thank you. I am happy to take questions.
